The problem

Trying a marble-run idea, or showing how gravity and bounce change motion, usually means scripting a physics engine or settling for a fixed simulation with a few sliders. I wanted the drawing to be the input: sketch a ramp, a ball, and somewhere for it to land, press Drop, and watch what happens. That puts three problems in one small app. A freehand stroke has to become a clean shape without a wrong guess. The simulation must not depend on the frame rate, or the same scene behaves differently on a slow laptop. And undo, save, and restart have to agree with what is on screen, even though Rapier, not the app, decides where each ball is.

What I built

AirForge runs in the browser. Dragging on the sheet with a mouse or a finger draws a stroke: a line becomes a ramp, a circle becomes a ball whose drawn size sets its radius (0.15 to 1.5 units), a rectangle becomes a platform that keeps its tilt, and a smooth bend or wave becomes a curved track. A stroke that matches none of them stays dashed until you choose what it becomes. A selected shape can be dragged, nudged, turned, or copied, each as one undo step. Drop releases the waiting balls, Restart puts them back where they started, and Freeze stops them where they are. Motion marks leave a dot behind each moving ball every 0.1 s and read out one ball's speed and height. Gravity, bounce, and friction sit in a World panel and are saved with the scene, which Save and Open move as a JSON file and Copy link packs into a URL. Eight examples, from a single ball and a cup to a curve racing a straight ramp, open from a menu or a link. An optional webcam mode draws with your index finger: raise it to draw, pinch to finish, and open your palm to cancel.

How it works

A stroke passes through four stages; moving balls report back every frame.

Input is a list of points in screen pixels, kept as drawn for a mouse or finger and smoothed for the webcam (src/stroke/capture.ts). A drag that stays within 6 px is a click, which selects a shape.

src/shapes/recognize.ts then tests for a curve, a line, a circle, and a rectangle, in that order, with geometry, not a trained model. A curve must be open, bend clearly away from the chord between its ends (12% of the chord to one side, or 5% to both, like a wave), and turn no more than 75° at any corner once wobbles under 6 px are ignored, so a nearly straight stroke falls through to the line test. A line's points must stay close to the chord between its ends. A circle's points must keep their distance to the center of their minimum enclosing circle within 18% of its radius, and the stroke must sweep at least 300°. A rectangle is the stroke's convex hull simplified to four corners whose angles fall between 70° and 110°. A weak circle that also passes as a rectangle, or a stroke that passes nothing, goes to the picker.

Shapes are stored in world units on the z = 0 plane. src/physics/world.ts turns them into colliders (boxes for ramps and platforms, spheres for balls, a chain of capsules for each curve, plus a floor and two walls), and Rapier steps them at a fixed 1/120 s with balls locked out of the depth axis. Each frame, every moving ball writes its position to a shared map that Freeze, Save, selection, and Undo read, so they act on the ball you see.

Key decisions

  1. Recognize shapes with geometry, not a model. Every test is a threshold I can read, explain, and unit-test (19 tests cover the recognizer), and none of it adds to the download. The cost is that a stroke outside the thresholds gets no answer at all, so AirForge leaves it dashed and asks instead of guessing.

  2. Step the physics at a fixed 1/120 s. Until 1.1.0 each step used the last frame's duration, so one slow frame became one large step and a different outcome. 1.1.0 fixed the step at 1/60 s, and 2.0 halved it, because at 1/60 s a fast ball could overlap the next pieces of a curve's bend within one step and wedge there. Now a scene behaves the same at any frame rate, and the headless tests step exactly what the browser steps. The cost: twice the physics work per frame, a slow frame runs several steps, and the ball is drawn between two steps rather than at either.

  3. Keep the scene as data and let Rapier own the motion. The store holds shapes and settings; Rapier holds velocities. Undo, Save, and Freeze read each ball's live position, so they match the screen. The cost is that velocities are never stored: a ball restored by Undo, or loaded from a file saved mid-flight, starts again from rest.

  4. Load hand tracking only when it is asked for. MediaPipe and its hand model download when the webcam is switched on, not with the app, and video frames are processed in the browser and never uploaded. The cost is that webcam mode needs network access to jsDelivr and Google Cloud Storage, and tracking shares the main thread with rendering, so fast movement on a slow machine can lose the hand.

What’s hard

Proving each example does what its note says. Each note is a claim, like “the ball settles in the cup”. src/examples.test.ts steps the same collider layout headlessly at the same 1/120 s and checks the claim for the saved scene and for eight to twenty copies with every ball nudged by up to 0.02 units, so no example passes on a knife edge. Before these tests, three of the old examples did not do what they described.

Repeatability has a limit. With a fixed step, one ball is repeatable: Ramp & Ball comes to rest at (3.37, −2.53) in the headless world and in three runs in Chromium. Twelve colliding balls are not. Of three Funnel runs in Chromium, one settled into a different pile, so the Funnel test only requires all twelve balls to land in the box.

Curves a ball can roll through. A curve collides as a chain of capsules whose neighbors share their end spheres, so the track has no seams. At 1/60 s a fast ball overlapped the next capsules of a tight bend within one step, and the contact correction threw it back. A headless sweep over the step size, Rapier's prediction distance, and the joint spacing showed that 1/120 s ends the wedging, while a larger prediction distance, the other fix, made balls lose more speed. Each joint still costs a little: in the Half-Pipe example a swing peaks about 20% lower above the bottom than the one before, damping included.

Webcam drawing that fails safe. A gesture counts only after three identical frames in a row, losing the hand for more than two frames cancels the stroke, and so does a fingertip jump of more than 160 px. Each rule trades a little responsiveness for never drawing a line the hand did not make.

Status and next

AirForge 2.0.0 is what the demo above loads, and GitHub Pages deploys it from main. On every push, CI runs 252 Vitest tests (unit, component, and headless physics), lint, a production build, and 16 Playwright browser tests in Chromium. The documented limits stand: shapes live in one plane, ramps, platforms, and curves never move, a ball loses a little speed at each joint of a curve, a scene holds at most 40 objects, and the JavaScript bundle is about 3.5 MB, 2.1 MB of it Rapier's WebAssembly inlined as base64. The webcam path has only been tested with Chrome's simulated camera. The next steps follow from those limits: test with physical webcams, move hand tracking off the main thread, and split the bundle.

Colophon

I keep this site to plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, served by GitHub Pages as committed, with no framework and no build step. Every word is in the HTML before any script runs; the scripts add the sculpture, the smooth scrolling, the search, and Gear Two. Fig. 0 stipples my avatar against a blue-noise tile and lifts the dots with a depth map; each dot carries a surface normal, so the light moves across the figure as it turns. The sheet has four columns whose lines stay put while the page scrolls, and every block starts on a line or just inside one, which a test checks at four widths.
